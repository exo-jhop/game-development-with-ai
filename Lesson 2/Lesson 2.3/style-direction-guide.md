# Lesson 2.3: Visual and Audio Direction

## What You'll Learn

When you ask AI to "make a character," you could get anything from a realistic human to a stick figure to a pixel art robot. The result depends entirely on how well you **direct** the AI — and direction requires vocabulary.

In this lesson, you'll learn how to talk about visual style, color, and sound the way art directors and creative leads do. You're not learning to draw or compose music. You're learning to **describe exactly what you want** so AI produces consistent, intentional results.

By the end of this lesson, you will:
- Describe art styles using precise terms AI tools understand
- Communicate color palettes clearly
- Direct audio and music style
- Write a complete style brief that keeps AI output consistent
- Avoid the most common mistakes in visual/audio direction

---

## Why Style Direction Matters

Without direction, every time you ask AI for an asset, it makes independent choices. Your player character might be pixel art, your background might be watercolor, and your enemies might be 3D renders. The result looks like a collage, not a game.

A **style brief** is your solution. It's a short document that tells AI: "Everything in this game looks and sounds like THIS." Once you have one, every prompt you write references it, and your game stays visually and audibly coherent.

## Art Style Vocabulary

These terms describe the overall look of your game's visuals. Use them in every art-related prompt.

### Pixel Art
Small, blocky graphics made of visible square pixels. Think retro games from the 1980s and 1990s. Can range from tiny 8x8 sprites to detailed 64x64 or larger.

*Sub-styles:* 8-bit (very simple, few colors), 16-bit (more detail, richer palettes), modern pixel art (high resolution but intentionally pixelated)

### Hand-Drawn / Sketchy
Art that looks like it was drawn by hand — visible line work, organic textures, imperfect edges. Feels personal and artistic.

*Examples:* Cuphead (1930s cartoon style), Hollow Knight (ink illustration style), Don't Starve (Tim Burton-esque)

### Flat / Minimal
Clean shapes, solid colors, no textures or gradients. Simple and modern. Think of app icons or infographics turned into a game.

*Examples:* Thomas Was Alone, Monument Valley, Alto's Adventure

### Retro
Deliberately evokes older technology — scanlines, limited color palettes, chunky fonts, CRT screen effects. Nostalgic and charming.

### Vibrant / Saturated
Bold, bright, intense colors. Everything pops. High energy and eye-catching.

*Examples:* Splatoon, Sonic Mania, Shovel Knight

### Muted / Desaturated
Soft, subdued colors. Grays, dusty tones, washed-out hues. Creates a somber, atmospheric, or vintage mood.

*Examples:* Limbo, Inside, Rain World

### Cel-Shaded
3D art with heavy outlines and flat shading, making it look like a cartoon or comic book.

*Examples:* The Legend of Zelda: Wind Waker, Jet Set Radio, Borderlands

### Vector
Clean, scalable shapes with smooth curves and crisp edges. No pixels, no texture. Mathematical precision.

*Examples:* Geometry Dash, Super Hexagon

### Painterly
Art that looks like a painting — visible brush strokes, textured surfaces, atmospheric blending.

*Examples:* Ori and the Blind Forest, Child of Light, Braid

## Color Palette Language

Color sets the mood of your entire game. Here's how to talk about it:

### Complementary Colors
Colors from **opposite sides of the color wheel** (red and green, blue and orange, purple and yellow). High contrast, energetic, attention-grabbing.

### Analogous Colors
Colors that sit **next to each other** on the color wheel (blue, blue-green, green). Harmonious, calming, cohesive.

### Warm Colors
Reds, oranges, yellows. Feel energetic, passionate, urgent, or cozy depending on saturation and brightness.

### Cool Colors
Blues, greens, purples. Feel calm, mysterious, sad, or professional.

### High Contrast
Big difference between light and dark values. Dramatic, bold, easy to read. Good for action games.

### Low Contrast
Small differences between values. Subtle, atmospheric, dreamy. Good for exploration or narrative games.

### Neon
Extremely bright, glowing colors against dark backgrounds. Cyberpunk, arcade, nightlife feeling.

### Pastel
Light, soft, desaturated versions of colors. Gentle, cute, approachable, relaxing.

### Monochromatic
Variations of a **single color** in different shades and tints. Unified and striking.

### Earth Tones
Browns, greens, tans, rusts — colors found in nature. Grounded, organic, natural.

> **Tip for AI prompts:** Instead of just naming a palette type, give specific color examples too. "Warm palette with dusty orange, deep red, and golden yellow" is more useful than just "warm colors."

## Audio Vocabulary

Sound is half the experience of a game. Here's how to describe what you want:

### Music Styles

**8-Bit / Chiptune** — Music made with simple waveforms, imitating old game console sound chips. Bleepy, catchy, retro. Think original Mario or Mega Man.

**Orchestral** — Full orchestra sound: strings, brass, woodwinds, percussion. Epic, sweeping, cinematic. Think Legend of Zelda or Final Fantasy.

**Ambient** — Atmospheric, textural, no strong melody or beat. Creates mood rather than driving action. Think of background soundscapes in exploration or horror games.

**Lo-Fi** — Intentionally low fidelity, warm, slightly fuzzy. Relaxed and nostalgic. Think chill study music or coffee shop vibes.

**Synthwave / Retrowave** — Synthesizer-heavy music inspired by 1980s electronic sounds. Neon, driving, energetic. Think Hotline Miami or Cyber Punk aesthetics.

**Electronic / EDM** — Beat-driven, modern electronic music. Energetic, precise, can range from ambient to intense.

**Acoustic** — Natural instruments, no electronic processing. Warm, human, organic. Guitar, piano, strings.

### Sound Effect Descriptors

When describing sound effects to AI, use sensory words:

- **Crunchy** — Rough, textured, gritty impact sounds
- **Clean** — Smooth, clear, precise sounds
- **Beefy** — Full, deep, powerful sounds
- **Tinny** — Thin, metallic, small sounds
- **Whooshy** — Airy, movement-based, wind-like sounds
- **Zappy** — Electric, buzzy, energetic sounds
- **Thuddy** — Dull, heavy, impactful sounds
- **Splashy** — Wet, liquid, watery sounds

## Writing a Visual Style Brief

A style brief is a short document that keeps all your AI-generated assets consistent. Here's what to include:

### 1. Art Style Statement
One sentence that defines the overall look.
> "The game uses a modern pixel art style with 32x32 character sprites, vibrant saturated colors, and clean black outlines."

### 2. Color Palette Definition
Name 4-6 core colors and when to use them.
> "Primary: deep sky blue (player, friendly elements). Secondary: warm orange (collectibles, rewards). Danger: crimson red (enemies, hazards). Background: soft lavender with darker purple for shadows."

### 3. Mood and Atmosphere
How should the game world feel to look at?
> "Cheerful and inviting. Bright backgrounds with soft gradients. No dark or scary elements. The world should feel like a sunny playground."

### 4. Consistency Anchors
Specific rules that keep everything looking unified.
> "All characters have 2-pixel black outlines. No gradients inside character sprites — flat colors only. Backgrounds use soft gradients but never sharp textures. All UI elements use the same rounded rectangle shape."

### 5. What to Avoid
Just as important as saying what you want is saying what you don't.
> "No realistic proportions — characters are chibi/super-deformed with big heads. No dark or muted colors. No photographic textures. No 3D-rendered look."

## Writing an Audio Direction Document

The audio equivalent of a style brief. Keep it short and clear.

### 1. Music Genre and Mood
> "Chiptune soundtrack with upbeat, catchy melodies. Each level has a unique track that matches the environment theme (grassy = major key and light, cave = minor key and echoey)."

### 2. Sound Effect Style
> "8-bit style sound effects to match the pixel art visuals. Punchy and exaggerated — every action should have a clear, satisfying sound. Jumping should sound bouncy, collecting should sound bright and rewarding, damage should sound sharp."

### 3. Ambient Sound
> "Minimal ambient sound. Light wind on outdoor levels. Dripping water in cave levels. No ambient sound on menu screens."

### 4. Audio Consistency Rules
> "All sounds should feel like they belong in the same retro-inspired world. No realistic or recorded sounds. No voice acting. Music volume should be background-level during gameplay, louder on menus."

## Before/After: Style-Directed Prompts

### Character Design

**Before:**
> "Make me a player character."

**After:**
> "Design a player character in a 16-bit pixel art style, 32x32 pixels. The character is a small fox with a red-orange body, cream-colored chest, and big expressive eyes. Art style is vibrant and cute with 2-pixel black outlines and flat colors — no gradients or shading within the sprite. The character should have idle, run, jump, and fall animation states. Style reference: similar to the character art in Celeste but with animal characters."

---

### Environment

**Before:**
> "Make a background for the first level."

**After:**
> "Create a side-scrolling background for a forest level in the same 16-bit pixel art style as the player character. Use a warm color palette: soft golden sky, rich green canopy, warm brown tree trunks. Three parallax layers: distant mountains (soft, desaturated), mid-ground trees (medium detail), foreground foliage (detailed, vibrant). Mood is cheerful and inviting — this is the tutorial level, so it should feel safe and welcoming. No dark shadows or scary elements."

## Consistency Strategies

### Reference Images
Collect 3-5 images that represent your target style and include them (or describe them) in every prompt. AI tools with image input work especially well with references.

### Style Anchors
Pick one finished asset that perfectly represents your style. Reference it in every future prompt: "Match the style of the player character sprite I already have."

### Negative Prompting
Describe what you do NOT want. This prevents AI from drifting into unintended styles.
> "Do NOT make it realistic. Do NOT use gradients inside sprites. Do NOT add 3D lighting effects."

### Batch Consistency
When possible, generate related assets together rather than one at a time. Ask for "the player character, three enemy types, and two NPC characters all in the same style" rather than requesting each separately.
